Thirty days, eight apps, and a name worth keeping.

Richard M. Davis, working largely alone with AI beside him, put a free open-source CAD suite in front of 25,000 downloaders in a month. Then he found out another project was using his name. This is his account.

November 9: the count

On the afternoon of November 9, 2026, the CadCraft homepage showed 297,106 people on the site, 109,333 app sessions in the browser and 25,202 downloads. The first month was over.

Richard M. Davis had not had time to watch it happen.

“I didn’t have any breathing time to go spread the word.”Richard M. Davis. He was too busy building.

October 9: a businessperson, not a programmer

Davis does not describe himself as a conventional software developer. He describes about three decades in business, database administration, systems architecture and high-performance infrastructure. Through The Data Design Group, Inc., he set out to build what he thinks professional design software should have been all along: capable, free, open source and responsive to the people who use it.

JavaScript made that practical. It got eight applications into a browser quickly, and into users’ hands. The open question was whether it could carry the weight of CAD for long.

The first pitch

Davis’s account of the name begins with a conversation, not a conflict. He says the idea came first, and that he took it to the team behind ArtCraft: two projects, compatible foundations, a shared direction.

What neither side expected, he says, was the moment both looked up and saw the same name on the door.

He says CadCraft was his first, and that he has since registered a trademark. What he describes after that is a collaboration he kept reaching for. He found little clarity about direction on the other side, and little appetite for cooperating.

October 12 to 24: the fork in the road

By October 12 all eight apps ran in the browser at version 0.3, with a custom DWG engine. As the work moved into modeling and technical calculation, Davis began to ask whether JavaScript could hold precision and performance over the long run. A private group of eight to ten insiders told him to keep the working product and experiment elsewhere.

Around October 17, as the project moved from 0.5 toward 0.6, he began a Rust fork. A C++ experiment followed. One reason, he says, was a possible path to closer technical compatibility with the project run by the ArtCraft team. He wants it on the record that this began before the trademark dispute, when he was thinking about alignment, not confrontation.

The experiments were expensive. Conversion work with Claude used up large token allowances and overwhelmed the virtual private server, so the work moved to a local workstation. After weeks, the Rust fork had roughly 0.5-level functionality with pieces of the 0.7 interface. The C++ effort was further behind.

After consulting the insiders, Davis paused the rewrites. Calculation accuracy and speed were real problems, but they did not justify converting the whole platform. The working product and the people using it came first.

October 25: a weekly rhythm, and a message from South Africa

Early on, CadCraft shipped about every three days: productive and exhausting. With 0.7 on October 25, Davis moved to weekly releases and gained room to plan and integrate.

Messages were arriving from outside the United States, in many languages. One conversation mattered more than most. A professional in South Africa named Duncan postponed a meeting, then connected with Davis over Zoom, heard the vision, and afterward wrote a detailed list of capabilities the software ought to have. He also described a familiar frustration with large software vendors: a feature request goes into layers of process and never comes back with an answer.

Users should know their requests are being weighed, and should have a real hand in what gets built.The conviction Davis took from that call.

November 1: the Great Eight

Version 0.8 arrived on November 1 as CadCraft Studio, and Davis gave the campaign its name. Drafting, BIM, modeling, review, quantities, site design, detailing and parts, eight apps with support for any connected AI and 54 languages.

For Davis this was the moment the software stopped feeling like a fast-moving experiment and started to feel like one product. Each app owns one layer color, the way a drawing set gives each discipline its own.

Command-line drafting and 3D solid modeling that opens and saves DWG and DXF.

BimCraft

Reviews and edits IFC models with 3D views, sections, schedules and clash checks.

Push-pull modeling for massing and early design, with groups, components and inference snapping.

Marks up PDF plan sets, compares revisions and runs team punch lists.

From Discord to development

Davis began building a workflow to turn Discord feedback into structured work: recognize a feature request, judge its relevance and quality, add it to the roadmap, and generate a technical prompt for an AI tool to implement.

When he described it, the process was close to that level of automation but not yet running end to end. Nor did he promise every request would ship within the hour, though some changes had been delivered quickly. The promise was smaller and harder to keep: suggestions should not vanish.

The name

As CadCraft grew, so did the overlap with a separate project managed by the ArtCraft team, which writes its name CADcraft. Davis did not let it halt development. He considered a distinct identity for a more integrated product, even whether the other project might keep the Cad Craft name while his own moved toward SuperCAD, and he reports pursuing trademark protection for SuperCAD.

He is careful about what that does and does not establish, and so is this page. His intentions explain his view. They do not by themselves decide legal priority, the other party’s motives, or the merits of any claim. The ledger below keeps the three apart.

What Davis says

  • Both sides discovered the shared name afterward.
  • He has registered a trademark, and has pursued protection for SuperCAD.

One window: SuperCad Studio

Eight apps in a launcher were not enough. Real work moves between drafting, modeling, takeoff, markup, visualization and reporting, and every handoff means a file transfer, fragmented data and another license. An insider suggested putting it all in one product. Davis built it: eight apps in one window on one project file.

It is first a product strategy: one workspace that shares data across every discipline. It also gives him an identity of his own, which is a useful second reason, not the only one.

The problem with success

Twenty-five thousand downloads and heavy traffic raised new questions. Davis began separating downloads from the web server, putting a content delivery network in front, and balancing load. That work came straight out of thirty years of infrastructure.

The same thinking applies to people. More users means more bug reports, ideas and expectations, and responsiveness has to be a system, not one person’s effort.

November 9 and 10: month two

By November 9, Studio 0.9 was out. A day later, the package that lets users connect their own AI landed on npm. On LinkedIn, where Davis has been sharing the project, the response was real, and it kept coming.

The uncertainty is stated plainly. Version 0.9 of every app is an early build, not finished software. Download counts do not prove sustained use. The naming question still needs careful factual and legal documentation. The aim has not changed: make professional design software more accessible, more integrated and more responsive to the people who use it.

The name may evolve. The architecture may change. The mission, he says, is larger than either.

The Data Design Group is just getting started.
